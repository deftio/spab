The lighthouse had not spoken in forty years, or so the villagers liked to say, but Mara knew better. Every evening she climbed the spiral stair with a pot of tea and a notebook, and every evening the old lamp clicked and hummed as though clearing its throat before a long story. She wrote down what she heard, though the words never came as words exactly, more as a pattern in the light, a rhythm in the long slow sweep across the water.

Her grandfather had kept the lamp before her, and his mother before that, back to the winter the tower was first raised on the black rocks at the edge of the bay. They had all learned the same thing on their first night alone at the top: that the sea keeps secrets, and that a patient keeper can be taught to carry them. You did not shout a secret across the water. You folded it into the spaces between the ordinary signals, into the pauses, into the small hesitations no passing ship would ever notice.

That autumn the storms came early and stayed late. Ships crowded the harbor, and the merchants grumbled about the fog and the freight and the price of rope. Mara listened to all of it, and to the quieter thing underneath, the message the lamp had been assembling for weeks, one faint increment at a time. She did not rush it. A message worth hiding is worth hiding well, and the best hiding places are the ones that look like nothing at all.

On the last clear night before the equinox she finished the transcription, closed her notebook, and set her hand against the warm glass. The lamp swept the bay once, twice, steady as a heartbeat, saying nothing anyone else could hear. Mara smiled and began the long walk down, carrying the secret the way the tower had taught her, tucked quietly into the spaces where no one thought to look.
